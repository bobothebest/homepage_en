> **Overview**
>
> Itô's formula is the chain rule of stochastic calculus. Compared with the ordinary chain rule it has **one extra term**, $$\frac12 f''\,d[W,W]_t=\frac12f''\,dt$$, and the reason is exactly $$[W,W]_t=t>0$$ from Part 02.
>
> In practice you only need a second-order Taylor expansion plus the multiplication table
> $$dt^2=0,\quad dt\,dW_t=0,\quad (dW_t)^2=dt.$$
> This post derives the formula, extends it to Itô processes, and uses it to build the standard stock-price model, geometric Brownian motion.

---

## 1. Why the ordinary chain rule fails

For a smooth path $$g$$, $$f(g_t)-f(g_0)=\int_0^tf'(g_u)\,g'_u\,du$$. The naive stochastic analogue would be $$f(W_t)=f(W_0)+\int_0^tf'(W_u)\,dW_u$$. Test it with $$f(x)=\frac12x^2$$, which would predict $$\frac12W_t^2=\int_0^tW_u\,dW_u$$. Compute directly with a partition of $$[0,t]$$:

$$\frac12W_t^2=\frac12\sum_j\big(W_{t_j}^2-W_{t_{j-1}}^2\big)=\sum_jW_{t_{j-1}}(W_{t_j}-W_{t_{j-1}})+\frac12\sum_j(W_{t_j}-W_{t_{j-1}})^2\ \xrightarrow{\Vert \Pi\Vert \to0}\ \int_0^tW_u\,dW_u+\frac12t.$$

(Check the algebra: $$W_{t_{j-1}}W_{t_j}-W_{t_{j-1}}^2+\frac12(W_{t_j}-W_{t_{j-1}})^2=\frac12W_{t_j}^2-\frac12W_{t_{j-1}}^2$$.)

The extra $$\frac12t$$ is the quadratic variation. So the chain rule needs a second-order correction.

## 2. Itô's formula

For $$W$$ a Brownian motion and $$f$$ smooth:

$$f(W_t)-f(W_0)=\int_0^tf'(W_u)\,dW_u+\frac12\int_0^tf''(W_u)\,d[W,W]_u .$$

Shorthand: $$df(W_t)=f'(W_t)\,dW_t+\frac12f''(W_t)\,dt$$. Note that "$$df(W_t)$$" by itself has no meaning — it is shorthand for the integral equation.

**Heuristic derivation.** Taylor to second order, $$f(x+y)-f(x)=f'(x)y+\frac12f''(x)y^2$$, with $$x=W_t$$, $$y=dW_t$$ and $$(dW_t)^2=d[W,W]_t$$:

$$df(W_t)=f'(W_t)\,dW_t+\frac12f''(W_t)\,(dW_t)^2=f'(W_t)\,dW_t+\frac12f''(W_t)\,d[W,W]_t .$$

This is not rigorous — a two-term Taylor expansion is not exact, and plugging $$dW_t$$ into it is formal — but it gives the right answer, and it is how everyone uses the formula.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Proof sketch (with the Taylor remainder made explicit)</summary>

Assume $$f,f',f''$$ are continuous and bounded. Write the exact Taylor expansion with a remainder,

$$f(x+y)=f(x)+f'(x)y+\tfrac12f''(x)y^2+R(x,y)\,y^2,\qquad R(x,y)=\frac{f(x+y)-f(x)-f'(x)y-\frac12f''(x)y^2}{y^2}.$$

$$R$$ is the "error per unit of $$y^2$$". By L'Hôpital twice, $$\lim_{y\to0}R(x,y)=\lim_{y\to0}\frac12\big(f''(x+y)-f''(x)\big)=0$$, i.e. the remainder is of smaller order than $$y^2$$.

Now telescope over a partition, with $$x=W_{t_{j-1}}$$, $$x+y=W_{t_j}$$:

$$f(W_t)-f(W_0)=\underbrace{\sum_jf'(W_{t_{j-1}})\Delta W_j}_{(1)}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\sum_jf''(W_{t_{j-1}})(\Delta W_j)^2}_{(2)}+\underbrace{\sum_jR(W_{t_{j-1}},\Delta W_j)(\Delta W_j)^2}_{(3)}.$$

- (1) $$\to\int_0^tf'(W_u)\,dW_u$$ (a stochastic integral, Part 03).
- (2) Split $$(\Delta W_j)^2=\Delta t_j+\big((\Delta W_j)^2-\Delta t_j\big)$$. The first part gives $$\int_0^tf''(W_u)\,du$$. The second, $$\mathcal Z=\sum_jf''(W_{t_{j-1}})\big((\Delta W_j)^2-\Delta t_j\big)$$, has mean $$0$$ (because $$W_t^2-t$$ is a martingale) and variance $$\le K(f,t)\,\Vert \Pi\Vert \to0$$, so $$\mathcal Z\to0$$.
- (3) $$\vert(3)\vert\le\max_j\vert R(W_{t_{j-1}},\Delta W_j)\vert\cdot[W,W]^\Pi_t\to0\cdot t=0$$.

</details>

**Two examples.**

- $$f(x)=e^x$$: $$e^{W_t}=1+\int_0^te^{W_u}\,dW_u+\frac12\int_0^te^{W_u}\,du$$.
- $$f(x)=\sin x$$: $$\sin W_t=\int_0^t\cos W_u\,dW_u-\frac12\int_0^t\sin W_u\,du$$. The $$du$$ term changes sign, so $$\sin W_t$$ is neither a sub-, super-, nor martingale.

**Rule of thumb:** the $$dW$$ part is a martingale; the $$dt$$ part is a drift. A function of $$W$$ is a martingale exactly when its $$dt$$ term vanishes.

## 3. Time-dependent Itô

If $$f(t,x)$$ has continuous $$f_t,f_x,f_{xx}$$:

$$df(t,W_t)=f_t(t,W_t)\,dt+f_x(t,W_t)\,dW_t+\frac12f_{xx}(t,W_t)\,d[W,W]_t .$$

**Heuristic.** Two-variable Taylor,

$$f(t+s,x+y)-f(t,x)=f_ts+f_xy+\tfrac12f_{tt}s^2+f_{tx}sy+\tfrac12f_{xx}y^2,$$

with $$s=dt$$, $$y=dW_t$$, and the multiplication table $$dt^2=0$$, $$dt\,dW_t=0$$, $$(dW_t)^2=dt$$. Why do the two mixed terms die? As $$\Vert \Pi\Vert \to0$$:

$$\sum_i(t_i-t_{i-1})^2\le\max_i(t_i-t_{i-1})\cdot t\to0,\qquad \sum_i(t_i-t_{i-1})\vert W_{t_i}-W_{t_{i-1}}\vert\le\max_i\vert W_{t_i}-W_{t_{i-1}}\vert\cdot t\to0.$$

### The exponential martingale (important)

$$f(t,x)=e^{\alpha x-\frac12\alpha^2t}$$ has $$f_t=-\frac12\alpha^2f$$, $$f_x=\alpha f$$, $$f_{xx}=\alpha^2f$$, so the $$dt$$ terms cancel:

$$df(t,W_t)=\alpha f(t,W_t)\,dW_t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{\alpha W_t-\frac12\alpha^2t}=1+\int_0^t\alpha\,e^{\alpha W_u-\frac12\alpha^2u}\,dW_u$$

is a **martingale**. The $$-\frac12\alpha^2t$$ is exactly the "Itô correction" needed to kill the drift that $$e^{\alpha W_t}$$ would otherwise have.

## 4. Itô processes

An **Itô process** has both a regular and a stochastic integral:

$$X_t=X_0+\underbrace{\int_0^t\Theta_u\,du}_{A_t:\ \text{finite-variation part}}+\underbrace{\int_0^t\Delta_u\,dW_u}_{M_t:\ \text{martingale part}},\qquad dX_t=\Theta_t\,dt+\Delta_t\,dW_t,$$

with $$\Theta,\Delta$$ adapted so that the integrals exist and $$X_0$$ constant. $$A$$ is never a martingale unless $$\Theta\equiv0$$.

Examples: $$\Theta\equiv0,\ \Delta\equiv1,\ X_0=x$$ gives $$X_t=x+W_t$$, Brownian motion started at $$x$$; $$\Delta\equiv0$$ gives a smooth process $$X_t=X_0+\int_0^t\Theta_u\,du$$.

**Quadratic variation.** Only the $$dW$$ part contributes:

$$d[X,X]_t=(dX_t)^2=\Theta_t^2\,dt^2+2\Theta_t\Delta_t\,dt\,dW_t+\Delta_t^2\,(dW_t)^2=\Delta_t^2\,dt,\qquad [X,X]_t=\int_0^t\Delta_u^2\,du.$$

**Integrating against an Itô process.** For adapted $$\Gamma$$,

$$\int_0^t\Gamma_u\,dX_u:=\int_0^t\Gamma_u\Theta_u\,du+\int_0^t\Gamma_u\Delta_u\,dW_u,$$

provided $$\int_0^t\vert\Gamma_u\Theta_u\vert du<\infty$$ and $$E\int_0^t\Gamma_u^2\Delta_u^2du<\infty$$. If $$X$$ is a share price, this is the P/L of trading $$\Gamma$$ shares. The result $$Y_t=\int_0^t\Gamma_u\,dX_u$$ is again an Itô process (a martingale only if $$\Theta\equiv0$$), with $$[Y,Y]_t=\int_0^t\Gamma_u^2\Delta_u^2\,du$$.

## 5. Itô's formula for Itô processes

For nice $$f(t,x)$$ (smooth, bounded derivatives):

$$df(t,X_t)=f_t(t,X_t)\,dt+f_x(t,X_t)\,dX_t+\frac12f_{xx}(t,X_t)\,d[X,X]_t .$$

Plugging in $$dX_t=\Theta_tdt+\Delta_tdW_t$$ and $$d[X,X]_t=\Delta_t^2dt$$:

$$df(t,X_t)=\underbrace{\Big(f_t+f_x\Theta_t+\tfrac12f_{xx}\Delta_t^2\Big)dt}_{\text{finite variation}}+\underbrace{f_x\,\Delta_t\,dW_t}_{\text{martingale}} .$$

So **a function of an Itô process is again an Itô process** — but the martingale property is not preserved in general (e.g. $$W$$ is a martingale, $$W^2$$ is a sub-martingale).

How to remember it: pretend the second-order Taylor expansion is exact, then apply the multiplication table, using $$dt\,dX_t=dt(\Theta_tdt+\Delta_tdW_t)=0$$ and $$(dX_t)^2=\Delta_t^2dt$$.

**Discounting** (used all the time in Parts 05–06). If $$dY_t=\Theta_tdt+\Delta_tdW_t$$, apply the formula to $$f(t,y)=e^{-rt}y$$ ($$f_t=-rf$$, $$f_y=e^{-rt}$$, $$f_{yy}=0$$):

$$d(e^{-rt}Y_t)=e^{-rt}(\Theta_t-rY_t)\,dt+e^{-rt}\Delta_t\,dW_t .$$

## 6. Geometric Brownian motion

Let $$\mu=\{\mu_t\}$$, $$\sigma=\{\sigma_t\}$$ be adapted with $$\int_0^t\vert\mu_s\vert ds<\infty$$ and $$E\int_0^t\sigma_s^2ds<\infty$$. A **(generalized) geometric Brownian motion**, written $$S\sim GBM(\mu,\sigma)$$, is

$$S_t=S_0\exp\Big\{\int_0^t\Big(\mu_u-\tfrac12\sigma_u^2\Big)du+\int_0^t\sigma_u\,dW_u\Big\},\qquad S_0>0.$$

It is the most common stock-price model. With constant $$\mu,\sigma$$,

$$S_t=S_0\exp\Big\{\big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\big)t+\sigma W_t\Big\}=S_0e^{\mu t}\cdot\underbrace{e^{\sigma W_t-\frac12\sigma^2t}}_{\text{exponential martingale}} .$$

**Dynamics.** Let $$X_t=\int_0^t(\mu_u-\frac12\sigma_u^2)du+\int_0^t\sigma_udW_u$$, so $$S_t=f(X_t)$$ with $$f(x)=S_0e^x$$ ($$f=f'=f''$$):

$$dS_t=S_t\Big(\big(\mu_t-\tfrac12\sigma_t^2\big)dt+\sigma_tdW_t\Big)+\tfrac12S_t\sigma_t^2dt\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\mu_t\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t .$$

$$dS_t/S_t$$ is the instantaneous return over $$[t,t+dt]$$. Since $$\mu_t,\sigma_t$$ are $$\mathcal F_t$$-measurable and $$dW_t\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal F_t$$,

$$E\Big[\frac{dS_t}{S_t}\,\Big\vert\,\mathcal F_t\Big]=\mu_t\,dt,\qquad \mathrm{Var}\Big[\frac{dS_t}{S_t}\,\Big\vert\,\mathcal F_t\Big]=\sigma_t^2\,dt,$$

so $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma$$ are the **drift** and **volatility** of returns. The $$-\frac12\sigma^2$$ in the exponent is the Itô correction again: the log-price grows more slowly than the expected return.

## 7. Application: integrals of deterministic functions are Gaussian

**Claim.** If $$f$$ is deterministic (non-random), then

$$\int_0^tf_u\,dW_u\sim N\Big(0,\ \int_0^tf_u^2\,du\Big).$$

<details markdown="1"><summary>Proof via the exponential martingale</summary>

Let $$S\sim GBM(0,\alpha f)$$ with $$S_0=1$$ and $$\alpha\in\mathbb R$$. With zero drift, $$S$$ is a martingale, so

$$1=E[S_t]=E\Big[e^{\alpha\int_0^tf_udW_u-\frac12\alpha^2\int_0^tf_u^2du}\Big]=e^{-\frac12\alpha^2\int_0^tf_u^2du}\,E\Big[e^{\alpha\int_0^tf_udW_u}\Big],$$

where we pulled out the non-random factor. Hence $$E\big[e^{\alpha\int_0^tf_udW_u}\big]=e^{\frac12\alpha^2\int_0^tf_u^2du}$$, which is the moment generating function of $$N\big(0,\int_0^tf_u^2du\big)$$ (for $$X\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$$, $$E e^{\alpha X}=e^{\frac12\alpha^2\sigma^2}$$). The mgf determines the distribution.

</details>

The variance $$\int_0^tf_u^2du$$ is exactly what the Itô isometry predicts; the new information is that the distribution is **normal**. We will use this for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process in Part 07.
